10.1 · Systems Are Everywhere

Systems at Work

The same stocks, flows and loops run businesses, bridges and bodies.
Business
Business · Supply Chains

The Bullwhip Effect

Procter & Gamble noticed something strange about Pampers. Babies use diapers at a steady rate, yet the orders reaching its factories swung up and down wildly. Each step in the supply chain reacted to the one below it, and every reaction arrived late.

The system inside

A balancing loop with a delay. Shelves run low, so the store orders more, but the shipment takes weeks, so it over-orders. Each stage up the chain over-reacts a little more.

Steady demand at the storeWild swings at the factory
Systems-thinking fix. Share real sales data with every stage, so nobody reacts to a guess.
Source: Lee, Padmanabhan & Whang (1997), “The Bullwhip Effect in Supply Chains,” Sloan Management Review 38(3).
Engineering
Engineering · Structures

“Galloping Gertie”

The Tacoma Narrows Bridge opened in July 1940 and was nicknamed “Galloping Gertie” for the way its deck rippled in the wind. On November 7, 1940, a steady wind of about 40 miles per hour set it twisting until it tore apart.

The system inside

A reinforcing loop. Each twist changed how the wind struck the deck, and that pushed it to twist even more. No single gust broke the bridge; the loop did.

Opened: July 1, 1940Collapsed: Nov. 7, 1940
Systems-thinking fix. Bridge designs are now tested in wind tunnels, so engineers can find loops like this before anything is built.
Source: Washington State Department of Transportation, Tacoma Narrows Bridge history.
Medicine
Medicine · Public Health

Antibiotic Resistance

Every course of antibiotics kills the bacteria it can. But any bacteria that happen to resist the drug survive and multiply, so over time the drug stops working and doctors have to reach for stronger ones.

The system inside

A reinforcing loop. More antibiotic use selects for more resistant bacteria, which cause harder-to-treat infections, which lead to even more antibiotic use.

U.S.: 2.8 million+ resistant infections a year35,000+ deaths a year
Systems-thinking fix. A leverage point: use antibiotics only when they are truly needed. Using less slows the loop.
Source: Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Antibiotic Resistance Threats in the United States (2019).

10.1 · What Are Systems?

Open Systems, Closed Systems & Emergence

A system is a collection of components that interact to produce outcomes that no single component could achieve alone.

  • Open for energy: Earth is powered almost entirely by incoming solar radiation.
  • Closed for matter: nothing crosses Earth's boundaries, so matter is used and reused endlessly.
  • Emergent properties: arise from how parts interact, so you can't predict them by studying components alone (e.g., the respiratory system delivering oxygen to the body).

Biosphere 2 contained miniature biomes: rainforest, ocean, desert.

10.2 · How Can We Show How a System Works?

Models, Stocks, Flows & Feedback

Stock
A supply of something observed over time (e.g., O₂ in Biosphere 2). A quantity that accumulates over time, like water stored in a lake.
Flows
Rates of change in a stock: inflows increase it, outflows decrease it (the faucet-and-drain analogy). The movement into or out of a stock, like deposits and withdrawals in a bank account.
Reinforcing Feedback
Positive feedback amplifies change. In Biosphere 2, falling O₂ weakened inhabitants, reducing their ability to fix the problem. A reinforcing loop is another name for a positive feedback loop.
Balancing Feedback
Negative feedback counteracts change and restores equilibrium (e.g., a thermostat). A balancing loop is another name for a negative feedback loop; it helps stabilize a system.

10.2 · How Can We Show How a System Works?

Two Ways to Draw a System

Stock-and-flow diagram

Purpose: to understand how systems change over time.

Stock: a quantity that accumulates over time, like water stored in a lake. Flow: the movement into or out of a stock, like deposits and withdrawals in a bank account.

Causal loop diagram

Purpose: to show cause-and-effect relationships in a system. The arrows indicate cause and effect: + means the two move the same way, − means opposite ways.

R Reinforcing (positive) loop: amplifies change, like melting ice and increased global warming. B Balancing (negative) loop: helps stabilize a system, like a thermostat regulating room temperature.

System structure mapping identifies the interactions among the components of a system.

10.2 · How Can We Show How a System Works?

Reading Real Systems

Case 1 · Stock-and-Flow Diagram

The Northern Cod Collapse

For centuries, cod off Newfoundland seemed endless. Then bigger trawlers caught them faster than they could breed, and in 1992 Canada closed the fishery, putting tens of thousands of people out of work.

Grand Banks, off NewfoundlandFishery closed: July 1992
Source: Fisheries and Oceans Canada.

Step 1 · The tool
A stock-and-flow diagram tracks what builds up.
Boxes are stocks, pipes with valves are flows, and clouds are sources and sinks outside the system.
Step 2 · The stock and its inflow
The cod in the sea are the stock.
Young fish growing up into the population are its inflow. That inflow depends on how many adults are left to spawn.
Step 3 · The outflows
Two pipes drain the stock.
The fishing catch and natural deaths. For years the catch alone was larger than the inflow, so the stock kept falling.
Step 4 · Behavior over time
A stock can fall a long way before it crashes.
Catches stayed high while the stock shrank, until there were too few fish left to fish. Decades later it still has not fully recovered.
Case 2 · Causal Loop · Balancing

Rush-Hour Traffic

Every weekday evening the freeway jams. When the trip gets slow enough, some drivers leave earlier, take side streets, or ride the light rail. Why does the jam stop growing, and why do new lanes fill back up?

Every weekday at 5 PMWhy new lanes fill back up
Source: Duranton & Turner (2011), American Economic Review.
toolbuildclosebehavior
Step 1 · The tool
A causal loop diagram shows what affects what.
Each arrow is a cause. Its sign says how the effect moves: + the same way, − the opposite way.
Step 2 · The first links
More cars, slower trips; slower trips, fewer drivers.
Cars → travel time is +. Travel time → people who choose to drive is −: a slower commute talks some people out of driving.
Step 3 · Close the loop
Fewer drivers means fewer cars (+).
Count the signs around the loop: one −, an odd number. So it is a balancing (B) loop: traffic settles at a level instead of growing forever.
Step 4 · Behavior over time
Balancing loops hold a system near a level.
Add lanes and trips get faster for a while, so more people drive, and the loop pulls travel time back. Economists call it induced demand.
Case 3 · Causal Loop · Reinforcing

Phoenix’s Heat Island

In July 2023 Phoenix hit 110°F or more for 31 days in a row, and some nights never dropped below 90°F. The city makes part of its own heat. How?

July 2023: 31 days in a row ≥ 110°FNights warm fastest
Source: National Weather Service, Phoenix.
toolbuildclosebehavior
Step 1 · The tool
Same notation, a new system.
Arrows are causes; + means the same direction, − means the opposite. Then count the − signs.
Step 2 · The first links
Hotter city, more AC; more AC, more waste heat.
City heat → air-conditioning use is +. AC use → waste heat is +: every AC unit pumps heat from inside to outside.
Step 3 · Close the loop
Waste heat keeps nights from cooling off.
Waste heat → night cooling is −; night cooling → city heat is −. Two − signs, an even number: a reinforcing (R) loop.
Step 4 · Behavior over time
Reinforcing loops amplify a push.
Each hot night means more AC tomorrow, which means a hotter night after that. Shade trees and cool roofs are ways to weaken the loop.
Case 4 · Both Tools Together

EVs and Charging Stations

People hesitate to buy an electric vehicle until there are chargers nearby, and companies hesitate to build chargers until there are EVs to use them. Which loop wins?

A chicken-and-egg problemCrowded chargers slow sales
Source: Li, Tong, Xing & Zhou (2017), J. Assoc. Environ. Resource Econ.
toolbuildclosebehavior
Step 1 · The tools
Stocks and flows, plus the loops that steer them.
The stock-and-flow diagram shows what changes; the loops drawn on top show why the flow speeds up or slows down.
Step 2 · One stock, one flow
EV purchases fill the stock of EVs on the road.
Every sale adds one EV. How fast the stock grows depends on what controls the purchases valve.
Step 3 · Two loops on one flow
Chargers pull buyers in; crowding pushes them away.
R: more EVs, more charging stations get built, more purchases. B: more EVs crowd the chargers, and crowding means fewer purchases (−).
Step 4 · Behavior over time
Which loop dominates decides the curve.
If stations keep up, the R loop wins and growth speeds up. If chargers stay crowded, the B loop slows it.

10.3 · Harnessing the Phosphorus Cycle

Phosphorus: The Slow Cycle

3×
increase in phosphorus flowing into the oceans
  • Building block of life: phosphorus forms DNA, RNA, ATP/ADP (energy transport), and phospholipid cell membranes. Unlike nitrogen, it never enters the atmosphere.
  • Human uses: fertilizer, detergents, and steel production. Phosphorus flowing into the oceans has tripled.
  • Eutrophication: excess phosphorus (and nitrogen) triggers algal blooms → algae decompose → oxygen depleted → dead zones harm fish.
  • Earth's phosphorus reserves are rapidly depleting from agricultural runoff and human waste.

10.4 · How Do Living Things Change the Nitrogen Cycle?

Making Nitrogen Usable

Nitrogen is essential for DNA and proteins, but 80% of the atmosphere is inert N₂, which most organisms cannot use.

Nitrogen Fixation
Bacteria convert N₂ into forms plants can absorb; it also happens via lightning.
Rhizobium Symbiosis
Bacteria in legume root nodules fix nitrogen, fed plant sugar in return.
Ammonification
Bacteria and fungi break down nitrogen in waste and dead matter, releasing ammonia.

10.4 · The Nitrogen Cycle (continued)

Returning Nitrogen to the Air

Once nitrogen has cycled through living things, other microbes convert it onward, eventually back to the inert N₂ gas that makes up most of the atmosphere.

Nitrification
Soil microorganisms convert ammonia into the nitrites and nitrates that plants take up.
Denitrification
Microbes underground and underwater convert nitrogen compounds back into N₂ gas, completing the cycle.

10.4 · Human Impacts on the Nitrogen Cycle

Too Much of a Good Thing

500,000
tons of nitrogen fixed daily by Haber-Bosch
  • The Haber-Bosch process chemically fixes N₂ into fertilizer: half a million tons daily, half taken up by plants and half lost to water and air.
  • Dissolved ammonium and nitrate are the leading contaminants of US public drinking water.
  • Excess nutrients from fertilizer (nitrogen and phosphorus) cause eutrophication and oxygen-depleted dead zones in rivers, bays, and the Gulf of Mexico.

10.4 · Reducing Nitrogen Pollution

What We Can Do About It

Most nitrogen pollution comes from how and when we apply fertilizer, so most solutions start there.

Time It Right
Apply fertilizer only during peak crop uptake, so less washes away unused.
Reduce Runoff
Minimize tilling and keep vegetated buffer zones between crops and waterways.
Engineer the Crop
Scientists are working to put nitrogen-fixing genes into non-legume crops, cutting fertilizer demand.

10.5 · How Are We Changing Earth's Carbon Cycle?

The Carbon Cycle

All life on Earth is carbon-based. Carbon cycles through all four spheres and drives climate change when human activity releases stored carbon (Chapter 11).

  • Photosynthesis pulls CO₂ from the atmosphere; respiration and decomposition return it.
  • Most of Earth's carbon is stored in the lithosphere (rocks).
  • When that rock is exposed to water and CO₂, it dissolves into carbonic acid, driving ocean acidification.

10.5 · Humans and the Carbon Cycle

Carbon Disruption: Fossil Fuels & Land Use

Fossil-Fuel Combustion
Extracts carbon locked underground for millions of years and releases it into the atmosphere. It is the primary driver of climate change.
Calcination (Cement)
Transforms limestone (calcium carbonate) into calcium oxide + CO₂. Cement production accounts for ~5% of human-caused CO₂ emissions.
Land-Use Change
Deforestation removes trees that draw CO₂ from the atmosphere, increasing greenhouse-gas concentrations.
Ocean Acidification
More atmospheric CO₂ dissolves into seawater as carbonic acid (H₂CO₃), making it harder for shellfish and corals to build calcium carbonate.
